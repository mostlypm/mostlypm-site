Every founder I talk to arrives with a solution. "An AI assistant for clinic intake." "A dashboard that tells sales managers who to call." That is normal, and it is fine. But a solution is only one expression of an idea. Phase 1 is about getting underneath it: who is struggling, with what, and why now.

This phase is short. Usually one or two workshop sessions and a few days of thinking. But it decides whether everything after it is useful.

## Why this matters more now, not less

With AI tools, it's tempting to skip straight to building. You can have a prototype in an hour, so why talk first?

Because a prototype answers "can we build it?" It never answers "should we?" When building took months, that question got forced on you by budgets and planning. Now nothing forces it. You have to choose to ask it.

## Step 1: The product workshop

I run the workshop with the founder and anyone close to the customer: sales, support, a domain expert. It produces four things.

**The product canvas.** One page that captures the idea's DNA: the problem, the target users, the value we create, the alternatives people use today, and the riskiest assumptions. The most useful box is usually "what do people do today instead?" If the answer is "nothing", that's a warning, not an opportunity.

**Key personas.** Not marketing personas with stock photos. Two or three real types of people, each defined by their job, their context and the decision they're trying to make. I push for specifics: "a front-desk coordinator handling 60 patients a day" beats "healthcare staff".

**Empathy maps.** For each persona: what they say, think, do and feel around this problem. This is where the real insight usually shows up. The founder often discovers that the pain they're solving is not the pain the user feels most.

**Workflows.** How the work actually happens today, step by step, including the workarounds. Spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups, sticky notes. The workarounds tell you where the product needs to fit in, and where an AI step could genuinely remove work rather than add a new screen.

## Step 2: Brainstorming on real signals

Once we understand the people and the work, we brainstorm solutions. But not from a blank page. I ground it in three inputs:

- **Customer signals.** Support tickets, sales call notes, interview quotes, usage data if a product already exists. What are people actually asking for, and what do they complain about?
- **Market.** What competitors and adjacent tools do, and where they leave a gap. Not a 40-slide analysis. Just enough to avoid building something that already exists for free.
- **Existing architecture.** If there is already a product, data or platform, what can we reuse? What constraints does it put on us? An idea that ignores the existing system is a rewrite in disguise.

I keep the brainstorm wide at first, then narrow it to the one or two flows where AI could make the biggest difference.

## Step 3: The intent note

The output of Phase 1 is a short intent note. One page, sometimes less. It says:

- What we're building, in one sentence
- Who it's for (the primary persona)
- The problem it solves and why it matters now
- The core AI flow we believe in, and what we're least sure about
- What we're deliberately *not* doing

The intent note is not a PRD. It's a compass. It tells Phase 2 what to prove first.

## What I've learned

- The founder is usually right about the problem and wrong about some part of the solution. The workshop finds which part.
- If we can't fill in the empathy map from real conversations, we stop and go talk to users. Guessing here is the most expensive guess in the whole process.
- "What are we not building?" saves more time than any other question.
